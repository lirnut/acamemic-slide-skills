# Select papers by evidence, not vocabulary

## Selection matrix

Before composing slides, record:

| Short title | Authors' question | Task and manipulation | Outcome measure | Role and boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paper A | What is actually tested? | What changes experimentally? | What is measured? | Direct evidence, extension, or boundary test? |

Use the matrix to test whether the papers answer a shared question. Shared words such as “agents,” “social,” or “collective” do not establish a shared phenomenon.

Prefer complementary evidence: a direct controlled experiment, a different behavioral setting, and a boundary/generalization test can make a better talk than three near-identical demonstrations. A boundary test need not confirm the first paper; make its different role explicit.

## Reject false coherence

- Do not equate convergence, conformity, correctness, cooperation, and consensus without operational definitions.
- Do not call any majority change “correction” unless ground truth is defined.
- Do not imply a common causal mechanism because several effects can be described using one word.
- Do not force a progression from individual to society if the sources do not support it.
- Separate an organizing question chosen by the presenter from a conclusion established by authors.

If papers are weakly connected, propose replacement or a narrower title. Do not repair evidence gaps with eloquent transitions.

## Outline gate

For each study, plan:

1. Problem: what is unknown and how do the authors frame it?
2. Approach: task, participants/models, conditions, controls, and outcome definitions.
3. Results: the smallest sufficient chain of original figures/tables.
4. Interpretation and scope: what do the authors conclude, and where does it stop?

Put the overview in short-title / question / approach form. End with separately attributed conclusions unless a cross-study synthesis is explicitly requested and supported. Do not append speculative proposals by default.
